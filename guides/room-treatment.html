What the problems are

  • Reflections. The voice reaches the microphone directly, and a few milliseconds later again off the walls and the desk. The reflected sound makes a recording hollow and distant, the “bathroom” sound. It is the most common problem in home recordings and the easiest to fix.
  • Background noise. A refrigerator, a computer fan, traffic, a furnace and the neighbors are recorded along with the voice. Treatment inside the room does little against noise from outside it; the remedies are the quietest room at the quietest hour, and sealing the gaps that let sound in.
  • Low-frequency rumble and boom. Small rooms add a boomy build-up in the low frequencies, strongest in the corners, and traffic and heating add a rumble below the voice. Bass traps reduce the build-up; a low-cut filter on the microphone or interface removes most of the rumble.

Treatment before equipment

A treated room improves every microphone, and an untreated room limits every one of them. The most effective changes cost nothing: record in the smallest, softest room in the house, away from windows and appliances, with the microphone a hand’s width from the mouth. A walk-in closet full of clothes is a good booth as it is.

Hard, parallel surfaces are the problem. Soft, thick materials close to the microphone and the reader are the solution: the nearer the treatment is to the reading position, the more it does.

Sealing out noise

  • The door. A door sweep or a strip of weatherstripping around the frame closes the gaps that let most household noise into a room. A solid door blocks far more than a hollow one.
  • The window. A heavy blanket or a removable panel over the window during sessions reduces traffic noise. Closing the curtains helps a little.
  • The house. Turn off the refrigerator, the furnace, air conditioning and fans while recording, and turn them back on afterwards. Move the computer out of the room, or as far from the microphone as the cable allows.

The noise print, and what cannot be fixed afterwards

Ten seconds of silence before a session give StellAurator a picture of your room’s steady sound, and the noise reduction uses it to tell that sound from the voice. The room’s live noise is drawn beside the saved print, so a fan switched on since shows before you record.

Steady noise, a fan or a hum, comes down afterwards. A sound under the voice does not come out cleanly: a dog barking or a door closing during a line stays in the recording, and the line is recorded again. In StellAurator such a line is marked for a pickup. Reflections do not come out afterwards either, and so treatment comes first.
