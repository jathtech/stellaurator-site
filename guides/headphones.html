Why closed-back

Headphones are worn for two jobs in audiobook work: hearing yourself while you record, and listening for problems while you edit and proof-listen.

For recording, the headphones must be closed-back. Open-back headphones let the sound out, and a sensitive microphone a few inches away records the playback, the cue lines and the click of a metronome along with the voice. Closed-back headphones seal the sound in and keep the room’s noise out.

What matters

  • Isolation. How little sound escapes toward the microphone, and how little of the room reaches the ears.
  • A cable, not wireless. Use wired headphones for recording. Bluetooth and wireless headsets play your voice back a moment late, which makes reading aloud harder; plug them in with a cable if they have one.
  • Comfort for hours. A book is recorded over many sessions of an hour or more. Weight, clamping force and the ear pads decide whether the headphones stay on.
  • A neutral, detailed sound. Editing is listening for small problems: a click, a breath, a harsh S. Headphones that exaggerate the bass or soften the top end hide them.
  • Cable length and a detachable cable. A cable of about 3 meters (10 feet) reaches the interface from a comfortable chair. A detachable cable can be replaced when it fails, which is the usual way headphones die.
  • Impedance. Headphones under about 100 ohms are driven to a full level by any interface. High-impedance models need a stronger headphone output.

Everyday headphones that also work

Many authors already own good headphones bought for music, travel or calls. A closed-back pair with a cable does the job for recording and editing when it is set up this way: the cable plugged into the interface, the headphones switched off or with noise cancelling off, and any EQ, spatial audio or sound enhancement turned off, so that the edit is judged on the plain sound. Each card below says exactly how.

Headphones that play through their cable only while switched on, with the sound passing through their own processing, are left out of this list, because the delay that processing adds is not published. Earbuds are for the final check of a finished chapter, described under proof-listening, not for recording.

Headphones for proof-listening

A proof-listen is a complete listen to the finished book, listening for mistakes, noises and changes in level. It is best done on the same headphones every time, and then checked once on ordinary earbuds and in a car, since most listeners hear audiobooks on those.

In StellAurator, Proof listen plays a chapter as it will be exported, including pauses, and ticks off each line as it is heard. A line with a problem is marked for a pickup, with the reason.
