What matters for audiobooks

An audiobook is hours of one voice, close to the microphone, in a room that is not a studio. Four things decide the result, in this order.

  • The room. Reflections and background noise are recorded along with the voice and cannot be removed afterwards. A closet full of clothes, a corner with heavy blankets, or a reflection filter behind the microphone does more than any microphone upgrade.
  • Dynamic or condenser. A dynamic microphone picks up less of the room and less background noise, which suits an untreated room. A condenser is more sensitive and more detailed, and records the room along with the voice, which suits a quiet, treated space.
  • USB or XLR. A USB microphone plugs into the computer and needs nothing else. An XLR microphone needs an audio interface (about $150) and gives more choice of microphone later. Several microphones below have both connections.
  • The noise floor. Audible's ACX requires a noise floor no higher than -60 dB RMS, peaks no higher than -3 dB, and an average level between -23 and -18 dB RMS. A quiet microphone in a quiet room meets this without effort. StellAurator measures every chapter against these requirements before export.

The room and the extras

The room comes before the microphone. A treated room improves every microphone on this page, and an untreated room limits every one of them. The room treatment guide covers what works at each budget.

  • Treatment before equipment. A closet with clothes on three sides, moving blankets on a frame, or a reflection filter behind the microphone removes the echo that no software removes. Carpet and curtains help. Hard, bare walls are the enemy.
  • A pop filter ($10 to $20) or the microphone's foam cover, between the mouth and the microphone, stops plosives before they are recorded.
  • A boom arm ($30 to $100) holds the microphone at mouth height without a desk stand that carries keyboard and desk noise into the recording.
  • Closed-back headphones ($50 to $150) so the playback does not leak into the microphone while you monitor. The headphone guide lists picks.
  • Placement. A hand's width from the mouth, slightly off to one side, at the same distance every session. Consistency matters more than the exact figure.

The interface and the booster

Every XLR microphone on this page needs an audio interface: it supplies 48 V phantom power, amplifies the microphone and connects it to the computer. A low-output dynamic microphone such as the SM7B or the RE20 also benefits from an inline booster in front of the interface. The interface guide compares more models.
